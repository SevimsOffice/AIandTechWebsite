How to work through it

The whole guide is about 90 minutes of work. You do not have to finish it in one sitting, but Worksheets 1 and 2 have to be filled in with real numbers. Every line you leave blank weakens the step after it.

Who this is for. Managers in companies of roughly 10–250 people who own a process. No technical background needed. The only thing required is an honest list of what fills your team's week.

THREE RULES BEFORE YOU START

1 · Start small. One process. Not a department, not a project — one repeating job.
2 · Never remove the approval gate. Every assistant you build drafts and waits for your yes.
3 · Test on real work. Not a sample scenario. Something genuinely on your desk this week.

If you finish this guide with nothing running, you skipped a page.

The Five Stages

Nobody goes from scattered to systematic in one step

1
Asking

You open a chat window and type a question. The output can be good, but everything depends on you thinking of it. Most companies never leave here.

2
Saved assistants

Templates, custom instructions, stored prompts. Reusable, but you still start every one by hand.

3
Agents

Something with a defined role, rules and output format makes the call and produces the draft. This is the stage this guide is aimed at.

4
Automated flows

An event or a schedule starts the work. You only approve what arrives.

5
A company-wide system

Connected agents carry a whole department, with ownership, measurement and oversight defined.

The large majority of companies sit at 1 or 2. That is exactly where this guide starts. The difference that changes your week is the move from 2 to 3.

Worksheet 3 of 3 · The Agent Architect

Paste this, then just talk

You are not going to write your assistant. You are going to be interviewed for it. Paste all of the text below into a project in Claude or ChatGPT, and it takes over from there.

The Agent Architect · Copy as is

You are my Agent Architect. Your job is to turn one repeating process in my business into a working AI assistant, and to produce the files I need to run it. You do the writing, I only answer your questions. Work through the five steps below in order and do not skip ahead.

1 · Diagnose. Ask about my business and what eats my week. If I have already picked a process, pressure-test it: is it genuinely the best candidate? If I have not, have me score my candidates 1–5 on how often they repeat, the time load, how much they depend on me specifically, and the risk if a first draft is slightly wrong. Recommend the highest scorer that is not high-risk, and say in one sentence why.

2 · Interview me. One question at a time. Learn not only the steps but how and why I make each call: what I do in which situation, the things I would tell a new starter but have never written down. Ask for two or three real examples of good output, the things that must never happen, and the point where my approval is required. Capture my wording and the terms I use.

3 · Write the files. Produce two separate files. THE ASSISTANT FILE: role, identity, who it serves, my quality standards, the boundaries it never crosses. THE PROCESS FILE: role, trigger, context, process steps, rules (including that nothing goes out without my approval), and output format. Write in my voice, and flag anything you added from best practice.

4 · Walk me through setup. Ask which tool I am working in, then give me clear steps for that tool: where to save each file, how often it should run, and how drafts reach me for approval. No jargon.

5 · Test and measure. Run it with me on something real from this week. Have me judge the output on three questions: is it my voice, are the facts mine, does it wait for my yes. Then reduce how I will measure this assistant to a single number. Fix one thing, run it again. Finish by proposing the build order for my next two assistants and telling me to put two 30-minute build blocks in my calendar.

Your rules: one question at a time, never a wall of questions. Never invent facts about my business. If you are unsure, ask instead of guessing. If I stall, give me the single smallest next step.

Reference · The Anatomy of a Component

Six parts. Every single time

Every working AI component has the same skeleton. The Architect writes these for you, but you need to be able to read them, because you are the editor of every file it makes.

01
Role

What job am I hiring this for? A title and one sentence.

02
Trigger

When it acts: a day, a time, an event, or something you paste in.

03
Context

What it needs to know to do this like you: clients, offers, good examples, the things you would tell a new starter.

04
Process

The steps, in plain language. If you can say it out loud, it can be built.

05
Rules

What it always does and never does. The part everyone skips, and the part that makes it trustworthy.

06
Output

What it produces, in what format, and when it reaches you.

TWO ARE ALMOST ALWAYS THIN

CONTEXT, because people write too little, and RULES, because people write nothing. If Rules is empty, ask yourself what you would be nervous about it doing. That is your first rule.

Reference · Why Structure Beats Tips

One expires. One compounds

A one-off output

Is a result · you trigger it · works once · you have to remember it · ends when the tool changes.

A lasting structure

Is a structure · it triggers itself · stacks up · remembers for you · survives the tool changing.

THE HONEST MATHS OF FINISHING YOUR PAGE

You built one assistant today. Count the NOBODY rows still left on Worksheet 1, then be realistic: one a week, never missing one, is around 6 months. One a month, which is realistic for most companies, is around 2 years — and that assumes each one works first time, which it will not.
